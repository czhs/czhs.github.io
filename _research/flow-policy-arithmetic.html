How a fine-tuned SmolVLA action expert adds and wraps a wrist angle

I fine-tuned SmolVLA to do one thing: rotate its wrist. The instruction reads "Rotate the wrist counterclockwise by 047.3 degrees.", the start angle b arrives in the state vector, and the policy has to output c = wrap(a + b), the target angle wrapped into [−180°, 180°), at every position of its 50-step action chunk. That is modular addition of angles, the standard toy problem of mechanistic interpretability, done by a flow-matching action expert that reads a frozen vision-language model's key-value cache. I wanted to know how the expert computes it, and whether it resembles the addition circuits found in small transformers and language models.

The expert's cross-attention looks each digit of a up in the frozen prefix, with separate lookup tables per head and per digit, and it reads a leading 3 as a negative angle. The two operands add by plain superposition, and the sum also forms inside a single query-key product. The modular step, wrapping sums above 180° back into range, is one attention head (layer 9, head 0) whose softmax flips onto the state token when the sum passes 180°; on cases that need it, removing that switch leaves 99% of first-step estimates and 100% of final outputs un-wrapped. The rarer wrap below −180° is spread over 24 heads. An MLP in layer 13 is part of the machinery that scales the velocity by 1/t, and every Euler step recomputes the answer, so the last step sets the output. All of this comes from one synthetic joint, with no images and a frozen backbone, measured mostly at the first flow step. I don't know whether a full VLA controlling many joints from pixels does anything similar.

The task and the model

SmolVLA (Shukor et al., 2025) pairs a SmolVLM2 backbone with a 16-layer action expert. The expert's odd layers cross-attend to the cached keys and values of the prefix (the instruction and the state token); its even layers attend over the prefix and the 50 action tokens. I froze the VLM, gave the model no images, and trained only the expert and its projections, 99.9M parameters. Numbers in the prompt have a fixed width, so every instruction is 15 tokens long and each digit of a always sits in the same slot. The requested rotation a is drawn on a 0.1° grid from 0 to 359.9°, and b uniformly from [−180°, 180°). Training ran for 100k steps, warm-started from a checkpoint trained on 12 discrete values of a. I analyse the best checkpoint (95k steps), which has a mean error of 0.68° on 256 fresh validation pairs.

The expert predicts a velocity v ≈ ε − A for the noisy action xt = t·ε + (1 − t)·A, where A is the clean action chunk, and sampling takes ten Euler steps x ← x − 0.1·v from t = 1 down to t = 0.1. Most of my measurements read the one-step estimate x̂ = xt − t·v at the first step, which plays the role that logits play in a language model: it is the model's current guess at the clean action. Unless I say otherwise, numbers come from panels of fresh random (a, b) pairs.

Two quantities recur. The model reads a leading 3 as −60°, so a request between 300° and 359.9° comes out as a small negative angle. I write a′ = a − 360 for a ≥ 300 (and a′ = a otherwise), and s′ = a′ + b for the unwrapped sum as the model sees it. Each case then needs at most one correction: subtract 360° if s′ ≥ 180° (the upper wrap), or add 360° if s′ < −180° (the lower wrap, which only happens for a ≥ 300 and b below about −120°).

Where the operands enter

Both operands reach the expert only through the prefix cache, but my first attempts at isolating a, on the discrete-a model, looked for it in the residual stream, as one would in a language model. The best of those earlier edits, a rank-16 patch aligned to the digit slots at layer 1, still left 23.9° of error. I then patched the positions a language model would use to carry an operand (the template tokens, " degrees", the final newline) at layers 1 to 8, even at full rank, and the output stayed on the receiver's answer. In language models, attention moves the information an arithmetic query needs to the final token (Stolfo et al., 2023). Here the language stream never consolidates a into a later token, and the expert reads it directly off the digit tokens' keys and values.

Transplanting keys and values works. Replacing only the state token's keys and values with those of a donor with start angle bd makes the model output wrap(a + bd) with 0.63° mean circular error, as accurate as feeding bd in directly (also 0.63°). Replacing the language tokens' keys and values gives wrap(ad + b) at 1.44°, and doing both composes at 1.42°, with 99% of runs within 5°. These numbers come from 72 fresh cases with three noise seeds each, selected and frozen before the final checkpoint existed. In the discrete-a model the same transplants also work between prompts with different numbers of digits, once the state token is rebuilt from a carrier prompt of the donor's length so that its rotary position matches (1.80° for a, 1.80° composed). Two controls on that model fail as they should: transplanting the language cache into only the even, self-attention layers gives 43.2°, and keeping the receiver's own state token and positions gives 107.8°.

This isolates a and b as input channels. It doesn't find a low-rank variable for a inside the expert, and it doesn't say how the expert adds.

Reading the digits

The frozen VLM's value vector for a digit is nearly the same in every slot (correlations between slots of 0.94 to 1.00 through layer 7), so the value says which digit is there, and the place comes from which slot a head attends to. Each place is then read differently.

The tens digit has one main reader, head 7.2, which puts 0.97 of its attention on the tens token whatever the digit and reads it through a nearly monotone table. Swapping only that head's read of the tens token for a donor's moves the output by 0.61 of the place-value change at the first step and 0.67 at the final output. The hundreds digit is read by digit-specific detectors, which is a lookup rather than a place-value readout: head 5.10 attends to the hundreds token only when it holds 1 or 2, head 3.7 only for 0 to 2, and each writes a digit-specific amount. All of the hundreds information enters through its own token (a transplant of that token alone transfers 0.99 of the change), and head 5.10 carries 0.35 of it by itself.

At the output, hundreds digit 3 is worth −57.6° rather than 300°. That is the negative reading of 3xx, and it explains why requests from 360.0° to 399.9°, which never occur in training, are answered correctly (median error 0.92°, 92% within 5°): a 3 followed by a tens digit of 6 to 9 reads as a small positive angle.

The ones and tenths are spread out. The ones digit reaches the output at full weight, but only about two thirds of it (0.68) arrives through its own token; the rest comes through other prompt tokens and the state token, which absorbed it inside the VLM. The tenths digit reaches the output at 0.86 to 0.96 of its place value, yet transplanting only the tenths token moves the output against the digit (−0.52 of place value at the final output), while transplanting the whole prompt moves it by +0.95. Overall resolution is about 1°, ten times coarser than the prompt's grid, and it is set by case-to-case jitter (0.57° RMS at the final output) rather than by missing digits: two requests 1° apart come out in the right order 93% of the time, 0.5° apart 75%, and 0.1° apart 55%.

Adding: superposition, not a clock

Once a′ and b are in, the expert adds them by superposition. In a two-way ANOVA over an (a, b) grid, at most 0.02 of the residual stream's (a, b) variance is non-additive after each of layers 1 to 8. The non-additive share jumps to 0.30 after layer 9, and from there on 81 to 87% of it is the wrap step itself, the indicator [a + b ≥ 180°].

One initial reading of this stage was wrong. From layer 9 on, many neurons have their Fourier power concentrated at the sum frequency (1, 1), and their tuning curve in a shifts one-for-one with b. That was first read as a trigonometric clock of the kind found in grokked modular-addition networks (Nanda et al., 2023) and in language-model addition (Kantamneni and Tegmark, 2025). Both signatures hold for any function of a + b, so they can't separate a clock from anything else. Harmonic ratios can. For the 475 sum neurons in MLP 13, the median ratio of power at (2, 2) to power at (1, 1) is 0.18; a sawtooth gives 0.25 and a sinusoid gives 0. The late neurons are ramps in a + b with a jump at the wrap line, copies of the wrapped answer. Causal tests agree. From layer 10 on, interchanging the fitted sum circle or helix between cases transfers at most 0.10 of the shift that interchanging the full residual transfers (0.92 to 1.00), and rotating a fitted circle did not rotate the output, even at the last layer.

The picture is closer to the account of Llama by Feucht et al. (2026), which adds cyclic quantities in base 10 and maps the sum back onto the cycle afterwards. In the action expert, one attention head does the mapping back.

The wrap is one head's softmax switch

Head 0 of expert layer 9, which I'll call head 9.0, applies the upper wrap in three moves.

It reads the sum. Take the head's attention logit for the state token minus the log-sum-exp of its logits for the two keys it switches away from, the hundreds digit and the final newline. On 512 fresh cases this gap is linear in s′ (R² 0.970), crosses zero at 176°, and weights a′ and b almost equally (60.4 and 63.5 logits per 180°). Against a + b as written, R² is 0.40. The sum forms inside the dot product: swapping queries and keys between cases shows that the query carries mostly a′ (weight 39.7, against 16.4 for b) and the state token's key carries mostly b (48.5, against 22.7 for a′). An exact linear decomposition of the query traces its a′ part to the digit-reading cross-attention layers 5, 3, 7 and 1.

It switches. The gap spans tens of logits, so the softmax turns its sign into a hard switch: attention on the state token is 5·10⁻⁸ when s′ < 150° and 1.00 when s′ > 210°, and the switch holds at every flow step.

It writes the correction. The state token's value, read out onto the wrist velocity through this head, is +5.16 readout units, against −0.42 and −0.97 for the two keys it leaves. Switching onto the state token therefore writes the −360° correction, overshooting it 2.6 times; later layers scale it down (below).

The causal tests run on fresh cases with s′ ≥ 180°, with every edit applied at every flow step. Removing the switch, by moving head 9.0's attention back off the state token, leaves 99% of first-step estimates and 100% of final outputs outside [−180°, 180°), landing 9.7° from the unwrapped s′. The rest of the network computes the plain sum; head 9.0 alone applies the upper wrap. Adding the switch to cases with s′ < 180° pre-wraps 79% of first-step estimates and 68% of final outputs.

One initial control for this edit was wrong. It permuted the edit across all key slots, which moved the change onto padding and onto slots the head never attends to, so it edited almost nothing, and its null result meant nothing. I replaced it with controls that remove the same kind of attention mass. Taking head 9.0's state weight and spreading it uniformly over the language keys un-wraps 99% at the first step (93% at the final output), the same as the fitted switch direction, so what matters is the state weight rather than where the displaced attention goes. The same edit applied to layer-9 heads 5 and 13, which also attend to the state token, and to head 1 un-wraps at most 0.6% at the first step. Reshuffling only head 9.0's language weights on cases with s′ < 180° degrades the answer (21° to 48° final error) but pre-wraps at most 12%. Cutting the state weight from 0.99 to 0.74, 0.50 or 0.25 un-wraps 5%, 59% and 99% of first-step estimates, so the layers downstream treat that weight as a binary decision with a threshold near one half.

The discrete-a checkpoint I analysed for comparison uses the same head. Its state attention jumps by +0.58 at a + b = 180°, its gap is additive in a and b (R² 0.99), and it reads 300 and 330 as +12° and +5°. It has two defects the continuous model lacks: it switches 12.5° to 17.5° late, and it reads "30" as −31°, because a leading 3 in that slot otherwise belongs to 300 or 330. In the 35 of 317 upper-wrap test cases where head 9.0 fails to fire, every final output is the plain un-wrapped sum.

The lower wrap has no single head

The lower wrap covers only 1.4% of uniformly drawn (a, b) pairs, and the model gets those cases right (1.7° final error). Three heads in layers 8 to 10, with three backups in layer 11, compute the comparison s′ < −180°; the three main ones read the same operand heads that feed head 9.0. Then 18 follower heads in layers 12 to 15 copy the step from upstream. Swapping the attention patterns of all 24 switching heads removes 78% of the wrap at the first step and 88% at the final output, and injecting their wrap-case patterns into other cases adds 87% and 90%; 24 random non-switching heads do nothing, and no small subset is enough. Removing the switches doesn't expose s′ either. The output folds onto the mirror image −c (a fit of the final outputs gives −362° − 1.04·s′), and I haven't worked out why.

Writing the answer at the right scale

With each token's final normalization held fixed, the wrist readout is linear, so the first-step answer splits exactly into direct contributions from the embedding and the 32 attention and MLP outputs (reconstruction error 0.7%). Regressed on a′, b and the two wrap indicators, the totals are 0.97, 0.99, −1.95 and +1.99, against targets of 1, 1, −2 and +2 in units of 180°. Attention writes each of these at 3.2 to 3.8 times its target. The MLPs remove a near-uniform 69 to 74% of what attention writes, and MLP 13 alone removes 50 to 59%.

MLP 13 was first read as trimming an attention overshoot. The follow-up showed that it is part of something the flow objective requires. At a fixed noisy action the exact velocity is v = (xt − A)/t, so the output has to respond to the answer with gain 1/t: 1 at the first step, 10 at the last. The network matches this closely (0.997, 1.25, 1.67, 2.50, 4.97 and 9.45 against 1, 1.25, 1.67, 2.5, 5 and 10 at t = 1, 0.8, 0.6, 0.4, 0.2 and 0.1). Attention can't supply the gain: its keys and values come from the prefix cache, which is the same at every step, and its pre-norm write stays between 8.65 and 10.26 readout units across the ten steps. The final RMSNorm contributes a factor of 1.9, and the MLP stack flips from subtracting (−5.52) to adding (+5.61). MLP 13 is the main attenuator at high noise. Relative to the answer entering it, it removes 71% at t = 1, 41% at t = 0.6 and 1% at t = 0.2, and at t = 0.1 it amplifies by 20%. The timestep does this by closing the gates of its answer neurons (for the 200 largest contributors, the mean SiLU gate falls from 0.29 at t = 1 to 0.04 at t = 0.2). The feedback is spread out: 239 of the 2,048 neurons carry 90% of MLP 13's answer write, and 98% of that write has the same, negative-feedback sign.

This changes how to read a standard Fourier ablation. Excluding MLP 13's sum frequencies raises the first-step error from 3.8° to 19.3°, about as much as mean-ablating the whole layer (18.9°), while keeping only those frequencies leaves 5.9°. In a grokked network that pattern marks the features that compute the answer (Nanda et al., 2023). Here it marks a gain stage, and the final output barely moves (at most 1.34°, against 0.95° unablated).

Every step recomputes the answer; the last one decides

With x̂t = xt − t·v, each Euler step can be written x ← x + (dt/t)·(x̂t − x). The weight dt/t rises from 0.1 at t = 1 to 1 at t = 0.1, so the final action is exactly the last step's estimate, and earlier steps can matter only through the noisy action xt that later steps read, which they barely do. If I swap in, at step k, the xt of a case whose answer is about 180° away, the final output still follows the receiver's own (a, b) in at least 98.6% of cases. The wrong xt pulls the estimate by 1.5% of the swap at t = 0.5, 7% at t = 0.2 and 23% at t = 0.1. The expert recomputes the answer from (a, b) at every step.

Only late damage survives as a result. Removing head 9.0's switch at a single step k ≤ 4 leaves the final error at most 0.8°; at steps 7, 8 and 9 it is 4.1°, 25.3° and 171°. Mean-ablating attention layer 9 at step 0, 3 or 6 leaves at most 1.0°, and at step 9, 120°. With MLP 13 ablated at every step, the estimate's error falls from 18.1° at t = 1 to 1.0° at t = 0.2 and the final output is nearly untouched (0.95° against 0.57° clean): gain control matters only at high noise, and the sampler's output is set at low noise.

Where the circuit came from, and what fails to generalize

The continuous model was warm-started from a discrete-a checkpoint, and head 9.0 was already a comparator there. Evaluated on the fixed-width prompts, that checkpoint's gap is linear in s′ (R² 0.92) but crosses zero at 225°, with a soft switch (state attention 0.72 above 210°). By 25k steps of continuous training it is calibrated (zero at 175°, hard switch), and after that only precision improves. The progress measures Nanda et al. used to find grokking phases stay flat across the continuous checkpoints (MLP 13's excluded-sum error between 19.2° and 20.5°, weight norm between 253.9 and 254.6), so there is no memorization, circuit formation and cleanup sequence to see. The circuit was inherited and recalibrated.

Held-out digits fail for a different reason. In four runs trained without hundreds digit 1 (weight decay 0.01 or 1, all or 5% of the remaining values, 35k to 40k steps), requests in the 100s never generalize (69° to 94° error). In the three checkpoints from those runs that I analysed, the comparator is intact (R² 0.95), but the unseen 1 is valued at 39° to 45° instead of about 100°. Unseen values built from trained digits do generalize, even when training sees only 5% of the values (3.2° error). The circuit generalizes; the digit value table does not. The same holds for hundreds digits 4 to 9, which never occupy that slot in training: the digit-gated detectors mostly stay closed for them, and the model misses by 86° on average. Start angles outside ±180° fail as well (66°).

Practical notes for intervening on a flow-matching expert

The tools from language-model interpretability transferred: activation patching, Fourier and ANOVA analyses and attention-pattern edits all found something. The mechanisms did not transfer, and neither did the usual intervention recipe. Four possible lessons for someone starting on a model like this:

  • Patch channels, not sites. The expert re-reads the prefix cache at every cross-attention layer, so a change at one site is overwritten by the next read. Patching all attention outputs from a donor transfers a and b completely (1.00 and 1.00), while any single attention layer transfers at most 0.13 and 0.26. Key-value transplants, cumulative patches and attention-pattern edits work.
  • Read the first-step estimate x̂ = xt − t·v to see the computation, and intervene at the last steps to change behaviour. The final output only shows what the last one or two steps need; the flow repairs earlier damage.
  • Pair a Fourier ablation with an output ledger before deciding that a component computes the answer. MLP 13 passes the grokking test and is a gain stage.
  • Check what your hooks and controls actually touch. SmolVLA adds the residual to the attention output projection in place, so an attention output captured without cloning is overwritten by the mid-layer residual; my early grid statistics for attention outputs recorded the residual for this reason (the ablations were unaffected). My first random-key control was a near-null edit for a similar reason.

Limits

This is a toy: one joint (the other 31 action coordinates are zero in training), no images, a frozen backbone and one policy family. Most measurements are at the first flow step with a shared noise sample, and the ledger is a linear direct-effect split. The lower wrap has no single-head account, its removal folds the output to −c for reasons I haven't traced, and the discrete model's handling of requests of 300° and above is only partly traced. The ones and tenths reach the output as the sum of many small, partly cancelling contributions that I haven't reduced to a readable mechanism. The larger open question is whether a comparator like head 9.0 appears when a policy has to get its operands from pixels rather than from digit tokens and a state value; this setup can't answer it.

Training and evaluation settings

Base policy lerobot/smolvla_base with the SmolVLM2-500M-Video-Instruct backbone, frozen. Trainable: the action expert and projections (99,880,992 parameters). 100k steps at batch 32, AdamW with learning rate 1e-5 (1,000 warmup steps, cosine decay to 1e-6), the native flow-matching loss with the wrist coordinate weighted 16 times, no held-out values of a. Prompt: "Rotate the wrist counterclockwise by {a:05.1f} degrees." The state holds b/180 in coordinate 4, and the target chunk holds c/180 in coordinate 4 at all 50 positions. Validation: 256 fresh (a, b) pairs with two noise seeds, every 5k steps. The analyses use the best checkpoint (95k steps). The discrete-a model used for comparison is a different checkpoint (155k steps) trained on a in {0, 30, …, 330} with natural-width prompts.
