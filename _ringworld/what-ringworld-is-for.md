Mech interp is one of the easiest fields in the world to start doing, and one of the
hardest to start doing *alone*. The papers are public. The models fit on a laptop. The
experiment you're curious about probably runs in an afternoon.

What's missing is a room and a recurring three hours. That's ringworld. Sundays, 4–7pm.
Two work blocks, a break in the middle where we talk to each other, and a show-and-tell
at the end for anyone who wants it.

## Nothing you do here counts against you

This is the part we actually care about.

Everywhere else, the work is graded. A failed experiment is a bad week. A dead end is a
thing you quietly don't mention. That's a rough environment for research that is mostly
dead ends — which is to say, all of the interesting kind.

So, here: a negative result is a result. Something you tried that didn't work is a great
demo. "I spent three hours learning that this can't work" is a genuinely good Sunday, and
we'd like to hear about it.

Come with something half-built. Demo at any point in the process — every project starts
somewhere. It doesn't have to be finished, or beautiful, or explicable to a stranger.
Nobody is keeping score.

## If you've never done interp before

Neel Nanda's [How To Become A Mechanistic Interpretability
Researcher](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/jP9KDyMkchuv6tHwm/how-to-become-a-mechanistic-interpretability-researcher)
is the best on-ramp there is, and its central advice is exactly the shape of a session
here: learn the minimal basics, then start doing research. Not after the reading list.
He calls mech interp "an empirical science", and reading about it is the trap most
people fall into first.

For a first Sunday, pick the smallest thing that produces a plot:

- code a tiny transformer from scratch — [ARENA
  1.1](https://arena-chapter1-transformer-interp.streamlit.app/) is built for this
- make a steering vector and push a model around with it
- run activation patching on GPT-2 Small
- train a linear probe on something you're actually curious about

Don't spend the first block deciding. Deciding is the failure mode. It's much easier to
be wrong about something that's already on screen.

And if two hours in it's going nowhere, drop it. Quitting early is a skill, not a
defeat — Nanda's advice is to pivot once two hours have gone by with nothing learned. You
have a whole other block, and the room is full of people with spare ideas.

## The one rule

Don't work on your main thing. No coursework if you're a student, no job work if you're
employed.

The whole point is the project you never get to. The moment the room fills up with
obligations, it stops being that.

## Interp is the centre of gravity, not a fence

Most of the room will be taking neural networks apart — probes, SAEs, attention
visualisations, that replication that's been sitting in a branch for a month. That's what
the room is best at helping with.

It's not a boundary. Writing, art, policy and startups are the same move aimed at
different systems, and they belong here on their own terms rather than by exception. If
you don't know what to work on, say so at introductions. Someone will have an idea.

## How to be here

Act like a host. Talk to strangers, pull the quiet people into the conversation, help
clean up.

Don't network. If you're here to network we will be so sad. Come to make things and find
extremely fun friends.

Bring snacks if you're feeling kind.

## Where the work goes

Whatever you make here can go up on this blog — finished or not, and the not-finished
ones are often the better read. Send it to us on Luma and it goes up.
