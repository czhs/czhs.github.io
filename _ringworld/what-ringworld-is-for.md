Mechanistic interpretability is an unusually easy field to start doing and an unusually
hard field to start doing *alone*. The materials are open, the compute is modest, and
the feedback loops are short. What's missing, for most people who want in, is a room and
a recurring three hours.

That's ringworld. Sundays, 4–7pm, on Craig Street. Two focus blocks with a social break
in the middle, and an optional show-and-tell at the end.

## The through-line

Interpretability is the habit of taking a system apart until you can say what it is
actually doing. Most of the room will be pointing that habit at neural networks —
probes, SAEs, attention visualisations, a replication that's been sitting in a branch
for a month. That's the centre of gravity, and it's what the room is best at helping
with.

It is not a fence. Writing, art, and startups are the same move aimed at different
systems, and they belong here on their own terms rather than by exception. The binding
agent is the method, not the subject.

## If you're new to interp

The single best starting point is Neel Nanda's
[How To Become A Mechanistic Interpretability Researcher](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/jP9KDyMkchuv6tHwm/how-to-become-a-mechanistic-interpretability-researcher).
Its core advice — learn the minimum viable basics, then immediately start doing research
— is exactly the shape a session here takes. The field is, in his words, "a fundamentally
empirical science," and reading about it is the trap most people fall into first.

Concretely, for a first session: pick the smallest thing that produces a plot. Code a
small transformer from scratch, or run activation patching on GPT-2 Small, or train a
linear probe on something you actually care about. [ARENA's
tutorials](https://arena-chapter1-transformer-interp.streamlit.app/) are the standard
on-ramp and are built for exactly this. Don't spend the first block deciding what to
work on — that's the failure mode, and it's much easier to be wrong about something
that's already on screen.

You don't need to be new to interp to come, and you don't need to be doing interp at
all. But if you are new, this is the fastest way in that I know of.

## The one rule

Don't work on your main thing. No coursework if you're a student, no job work if you're
employed. The whole point is the project you never get to, and the moment the room fills
up with obligations it stops being that.

If you don't know what to work on, say so when we do introductions. Someone will have an
idea.

## Everything else

Act like a host. Include people in conversations, talk to strangers, offer to help out
and clean up. Don't network — if you're here to network we will be so sad. Come to make
stuff and find super fun friends.

Bring snacks if you're feeling kind.

The format is borrowed, with gratitude, from the [Socratica
toolbox](https://toolbox.socratica.info/), which is the best writing I know of on how to
run a room like this. ringworld is hosted by [CASI, the CMU AI Safety
Initiative](https://cmuaisafety.com/).

Whatever you make here can go up on this blog. That's what it's for.
