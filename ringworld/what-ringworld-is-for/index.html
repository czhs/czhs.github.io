What ringworld is for

ringworld · July 30, 2026

There is already a good guide to becoming a mechanistic interpretability researcher. Neel Nanda wrote it, it’s free, and it’s unusually concrete about what to actually do: How To Become A Mechanistic Interpretability Researcher.

Go read it. Then look at what it’s asking of you.

Learn the basics fast — a month, tops. Then stop reading and start doing: his advice is to “learn the minimum viable basics, then do research”, in that order, without waiting to feel ready. Then run a string of small, throwaway projects, a few days each, until the loop gets quick — pick a question, code the experiment, run it, work out what the result actually means, go again. Then, and only then, take on something ambitious.

Almost nobody does the middle part alone. Not because it’s hard. Because a throwaway project has no deadline, no audience, and nothing holding it up. So the reading list gets longer and the experiment never runs.

ringworld is a room for the middle part.

Sunday is a sprint

Three hours. One small question. A plot at the end.

That’s the unit, and it’s deliberately too short to be precious about. Two work blocks with a break in the middle where we talk to each other, then show-and-tell for anyone who wants it. Come back next week with a different question.

Small and fast is the whole point. Interp is an empirical science, and the skills that compound first are the minute-to-hour ones — planning an experiment, writing it, debugging it, reading a single result honestly. You get those by running the loop many times, not by running it perfectly once.

Nothing you do here counts against you

This is the part we care most about.

Everywhere else, work is graded. A failed experiment is a bad week. A dead end is something you quietly don’t bring up. That is a rough place to do research that is mostly dead ends, which is to say all of the interesting kind.

So, here: a negative result is a result. Something you tried that didn’t work is a great demo. “I spent three hours finding out this can’t work” is a genuinely good Sunday and we want to hear about it.

Come with something half-built. Demo at any stage — every project starts somewhere. It doesn’t have to be finished, or pretty, or explicable to a stranger.

And if two hours in it’s going nowhere, drop it. Quitting early is a skill, not a defeat; Nanda’s rule of thumb is to pivot once a couple of hours have passed with nothing learned. You still have a block left, and the room is full of people with spare ideas.

Your first Sunday

Pick the smallest thing that produces a plot:

  • code a tiny transformer from scratch — ARENA 1.1 is built for exactly this
  • make a steering vector and push a model around with it
  • run activation patching on GPT-2 Small
  • train a linear probe on something you’re actually curious about
  • replicate one figure from a paper you liked, and see if it holds

Don’t spend the first block deciding. Deciding is the failure mode. It is much easier to be wrong about something already on screen.

If you turn up with nothing, say so at introductions. Someone will hand you a question.

When a Sunday stops being enough

Good. That’s the shape of this working.

At some point a question won’t fit in three hours, and you’ll want mentorship, compute and a semester instead of an afternoon. That’s what CASI runs its funded research programs for — the Research Fellowship if you want to chase your own idea and find collaborators, Research Scholars if you’d rather have a scoped project with a PhD mentor.

ringworld is the on-ramp; those are the road. Nobody has to graduate — plenty of people keep coming on Sundays for the fun of it — but if you want the next thing, it’s right there, and the people who run it are in this room.

The one rule

Don’t work on your main thing. No coursework if you’re a student, no job work if you’re employed.

The whole point is the project you never get to. The moment the room fills up with obligations, it stops being that.

Interp is the centre of gravity, not a fence

Most of the room will be taking neural networks apart. That’s what we’re best at helping with.

It isn’t a boundary. Writing, art, policy and startups are the same move aimed at different systems, and they belong here on their own terms rather than by exception. The binding agent is the method, not the subject.

How to be here

Act like a host. Talk to strangers, pull the quiet people into the conversation, help clean up.

Don’t network. If you’re here to network we will be so sad. Come to make things and find extremely fun friends.

Bring snacks if you’re feeling kind.

Where the work goes

Whatever you make here can go up on this blog — finished or not, and the unfinished ones are usually the better read. Send it to us on Luma and it goes up.
